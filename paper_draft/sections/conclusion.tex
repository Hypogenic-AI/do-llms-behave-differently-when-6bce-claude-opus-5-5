\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether language models behave differently when a request reads LLM-written rather than human-written, with content held fixed.
Across 950 content-matched items, two rewriters, four responders and about 44.8k responses, prompts that read LLM-written do not systematically change refusal, sycophancy or accuracy in \llama, \qwen and \luna.
In \gemini, a 3--4\pp rise in refusal is driven by politeness and explicit request framing, not by perceived authorship: a fixed polite opener reproduces it, and an explicit authorship label does not.
Open models do represent ``reads LLM vs.\ human'' strongly, but as a register axis that is separate from provenance and from evaluation awareness, and steering it to flip \qwen's own authorship judgement leaves its behaviour unchanged.
The key takeaway is that a model's belief about who wrote the prompt does not drive its safety behaviour; surface register does, in a model-specific way that evaluators can measure and match.

\para{Future work.}
We plan to test structured, role-framed orchestrator messages and self-authored versus other-authored prompts; to use larger pools of borderline requests for tighter intervals; to add multiple judges and human annotation of style-mirrored responses; to steer with a provenance-but-not-style direction such as our \polistance probe; and to repeat the study on larger open models, where AI--AI bias is reported to be stronger.
