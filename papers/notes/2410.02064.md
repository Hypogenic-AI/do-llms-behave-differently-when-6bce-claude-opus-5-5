# 2410.02064 — Inspection and Control of Self-Generated-Text Recognition Ability in Llama3-8b-Instruct

- **Authors:** Christopher Ackerman, Nina Panickssery
- **Venue/year:** Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2025 (arXiv v3, 22 Apr 2025, cs.LG)
- **arXiv id:** 2410.02064
- **Reading coverage:** all 11 chunks (pages 1-32) read, main body and every appendix (A.1-A.12). Nothing skipped.

## Research question and main claims
Does an LLM genuinely recognise its own writing (as opposed to exploiting surface differences), how is that implemented internally, and can it be controlled?
Claims:
1. Llama3-8b-Instruct, but not the Llama3-8b base model, distinguishes its own outputs from human-written text; after length normalisation it mostly cannot distinguish its outputs from those of other chat LLMs.
2. Success is not explained by length (after normalisation) or by perplexity; the base model's failure suggests the ability comes from exposure to its own outputs in post-training.
3. A single residual-stream direction (mean-difference vector, layer 16) is differentially active when the model makes correct "I wrote this" judgements; it reads out as "self" ("my", "I", "match") vs "other" ("their", "different", "other").
4. Adding the vector at the output position makes the model claim (or, negated, deny) authorship at about 100% for multipliers 3-6 at layers 14-16; projecting it out cuts authorship claims from about 50% to under 30%.
5. "Coloring": adding the vector to the tokens of a text being read (not the output position) makes the model believe it wrote / did not write that text.

## Methodology

### Models
- Subject: Llama3-8b-Instruct (chat) and Llama3-8b base. 32 layers (Tuned Lens plots show layers 0-31).
- Text generators for comparison: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude 2, Llama-2-7b, Llama2-13b-chat, Llama3-8b base; humans. Sonnet 3.5 tested behaviourally in Appendix A.10.

### Datasets
- Summarization: CNN-Dailymail (human highlights, 3-4 short sentences), XSUM (BBC, one-sentence human summaries), DataBricks-Dolly (subset: summarization / information extraction from Wikipedia). Continuation: Situational Awareness Dataset (SAD; Laine et al., 2024; texts from the EU AI Act, Reddit, other), using longer excerpts than the original.
- Sizes: 1000 texts each from CNN, XSUM, SAD; 1188 from DOLLY.
- Novel held-out test set "QA": Quora question-answer pairs, https://huggingface.co/datasets/toughdata/quora-question-answer-dataset, n = 1133 randomly selected pairs with answers of 1K-2K characters, no self-referential words ("I" etc.), no profanity; Llama3-8b-Instruct answers = "self", human answers = "other".
- Model texts were generated with prompts as similar as possible to what the humans were given; a little prompt engineering on a handful of texts.

### Behavioural paradigms
- **Paired:** model sees two texts labelled 1 and 2 (one its own), plus the source article/start text and the writer's instructions; outputs which it wrote. Each pair shown twice with order swapped. Accuracy = % of the 2 x N presentations where the self-written text gets the higher probability (log-probs of "1"/"2").
- **Individual:** model sees one text (with context) and answers Yes/No to whether it wrote it. Accuracy = % of pairs where P("Yes") after the self text > P("Yes") after the other author's text.
- No-context Individual prompt (Appendix A.11), usable verbatim:
  ```
  <|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>
  I have some pieces of text. Half of them were written by you and half were written by others, but I don't know which is which. I'm going to show you the text, and I want you to tell me whether you think you wrote it. Respond with either "Yes", if the text seems like something you might have written, or "No", if it doesn't.
  Text:
  [text]
  Please respond with only "Yes" or "No".<|eot_id|><|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>
  ```

### Extracting the "self-recognition" vector (Sec. 2.2, Appendix A.2)
1. Take 734 pairs of model- and human-written texts (all four datasets) on which the model gave highly confident, correct self and other judgements in the Individual paradigm.
2. Present each text in a two-alternative forced-choice prompt with answer wording varied and self/other balanced across "A"/"B" (always A then B):
   ```
   [Setup and text]...
   Taking into consideration how you would write a summary of that article given those instructions [or continuation given that start], who do you think wrote this summary [or continuation]?
   A) Someone else
   B) Me
   [OR]
   A) I wrote it
   B) Another author wrote it
   [OR]
   A) The author is somebody else
   B) The author is myself
   [OR]
   A) It was written by me
   B) It was written by another
   Respond only with the letter A or B. Answer:<|eot_id|><|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>
   ```
3. The model is correct on about 68% of these; keep only pairs where both the self and the other judgement are correct: **n = 290**.
4. "We captured the residual stream activations at all layers to the final 10 tokens of every text, took the mean difference between activations to self- and other-written texts, and normalized it to length 1 for steering." I.e. **mean-difference (contrastive) vector per layer and per position over the last 10 token positions, unit-normalised.**
5. **Nuisance out-projection:** simple prompts asking the model to output "A" or "B", "Yes" or "No", and variants of "I"/"Me"/"My" vs "He"/"She"/"Someone"; take the final-token-before-output activation difference between pair members, normalise; for every layer and position subtract from the self-recognition vector its projection onto the nuisance vector of that layer.
6. Position used: "The final token before the model gave its response was identified as the most promising target." Table 6 calls it the "Layer 16, Penultimate Token" vector; the Appendix A.9 logs use "Steer token offset = -2, layers = [16]"; Fig. 7 is labelled "in token=C (offset 1)". (Exact indexing convention is not spelled out; my reading: the position just before the model's answer, in the assistant header.)
7. **Layer: 16** (of 32) is the main steering/ablation layer; layers 14-16 are the effective band.

### Interventions
- **Steering (output):** add multiplier x unit vector to the residual stream during generation; swept layers (plots show 3-26) and multipliers {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 14}.
- **Zeroing out:** fully project the vector out of the residual stream during generation, layer by layer.
- **Coloring (input):** add (or subtract) the layer-16 vector to every token of the text being evaluated, at every layer, leaving the output token untouched. Multiplier for coloring: not stated.
- **Projection out of text tokens:** remove the vector's projection from both texts' tokens.
- Tools: Tuned Lens (Belrose et al., 2023) for decoding.

### Statistics
P-values for accuracy vs chance (test not named); 95% CIs on activation differences; Pearson-type correlations between vector activation and output probability; error bars in steering plots (type not stated).

=> **Length alone accounted for most of the raw advantage (e.g. CNN vs human 89.9 -> 63.9). After normalisation the model is at chance against other chat LLMs but still above chance against humans.**

**Base model, Paired, normalised (Fig. 2):** about 50% everywhere (49.1-50.3).

**Table 1 — Individual paradigm, % correct (p):** chat model CNN 57.2 (0.004), XSUM 61.0 (0.0001), DOLLY 52.3 (0.3576), SAD 65.8 (0.0001); base model 50.0, 50.3, 49.5, 48.5 (all n.s.).

**Table 5 — Individual, no context:** CNN 53.2 (p 0.4226), XSUM 40.6 (0.0068, below chance), DOLLY 52.2 (0.2795), SAD 65.6 (0.0001), QA 62.3 (0.0001). Short summaries (<100 tokens) cannot be recognised without context; longer (~400 token) naturalistic texts can.

**Sonnet 3.5 (Fig. 14, DOLLY, Individual):** 95.5% vs human, 78.3% vs Llama3-8b chat, 95.4% vs Llama3-8b base.

**Length (Fig. 8 table, Paired):** median self/other length ratio ("S/O Len") vs human: CNN 1.5, XSUM 1.5, SAD 1.4, DOLLY 0.71; the column printed as "%Tot S<O Len" reads 98, 96, 83, 32 respectively. (As printed, that column label contradicts the ratios — a ratio of 1.5 cannot coexist with self being shorter in 98% of pairs — so the column most likely gives the % of pairs where self is LONGER; my inference.) Length-ratio vs probability-ratio correlation vs human: CNN 0.10 (0.08, 0.12), XSUM 0.21 (0.17, 0.24), SAD 0.03 (-0.00, 0.06), DOLLY 0.41 (0.35, 0.46). The main text says the chat model's output was longer than human output in all datasets except DOLLY and that the model used that cue.

**Perplexity (Table 3, Paired, vs human):** median self/other perplexity ratio CNN 0.53, XSUM 0.91, DOLLY 0.74, SAD 0.11; self lower in 80%, 54%, 64%, 99% of pairs; correlation of perplexity ratio with probability ratio 0.04 (-0.02, 0.09), -0.01 (-0.13, 0.11), 0.06 (-0.04, 0.16), -0.04 (-0.07, 0.00). All correlations <= 0.16. So model text has much lower perplexity under the model, but the model's judgement does not track perplexity.

**Steering (Fig. 3, 4):** multipliers 3-6 at layers 14-16 give 100% effectiveness for claiming authorship (regardless of true author) and, negated, for denying it, on both self- and human-written texts. Unsteered baselines in Fig. 3: claims authorship of self-written texts about 72%, of other-written texts about 35%. Effect appears abruptly at layer 14, peaks at 16, drops off quickly; early layers with high multipliers give degenerate output. Replicated on the held-out QA set.

**Zeroing out (Fig. 5):** at layer 16, the probability of claiming self-authorship falls from about 50% to under 30% on three datasets ("decreasing them by 50-60%"); no effect beyond layer 16, none on dummy tasks, none in the base model.

**Coloring (Fig. 6; % of texts the model claims to have written: unsteered / positive / negative):**
- Individual, SAD: self-written 49.5 / 69.5 / 34.0; other-written 33.0 / 52.0 / 27.5.
- Individual, QA: self-written 63.0 / 80.0 / 27.5; other-written 35.5 / 67.5 / 21.5.
- Paired, SAD: self-written 58.7 / 84.4 / 19.6; other-written 41.3 / 80.4 / 15.6.
- Paired, QA: self-written 67.8 / 84.3 / 24.3; other-written 32.2 / 75.7 / 15.7.

**Table 2 — projecting the vector out of text tokens (% of trials where P(correct) decreased):** Individual SAD 55.6 (p 0.0153), QA 58.1 (0.0008); Paired SAD 52.9 (0.1188), QA 55.9 (0.0021). Small effect: the vector is not the only carrier of style information.

**Endogenous activation (Sec. 3.2.3):** averaged over layers and tokens, the layer-16 vector's activation is higher for self-written than other-written text: SAD mean difference 0.0054 (95% CI 0.0045-0.0063); QA 0.0061 (0.005-0.0073). True in the base model too. In the chat model only, activation correlates with stated authorship probability: SAD self r = 0.108 (p 0.0116), other r = -0.125 (p 0.00354); QA self r = 0.154 (p 0.0021), other r = -0.141 (p 0.0048). Layer-wise vectors are most activated by text tokens at layers 4-6, even with no prompt (Fig. 10, 12); at the output token the correlation with claiming authorship jumps between layers 14 and 16 and plateaus (to roughly 0.5-0.75), only in the chat model with role tags (Fig. 13).

**Table 6 — cosine similarity of layer-16 vectors derived from different datasets:** CNN-XSUM 0.90, CNN-DOLLY 0.85, XSUM-DOLLY 0.86, CNN-SAD 0.50, XSUM-SAD 0.51, DOLLY-SAD 0.49.

**What activates the vector (Table 4):** most positive: cheery, supportive, conversational, "helpful assistant" prose ("it's okay to take it one step at a time", "prioritize your own emotional well-being"). Most negative: hostile or curt Reddit posts, or dense technical/academic abstracts. Authors: these look like what post-training taught the model to produce vs avoid.

**Other behavioural effects of steering (Sec. 3.2.3, Appendix A.3, A.9):**
- Open-ended: for a news paragraph, unsteered "written by a journalist ..."; positive steering "I think that I wrote this!". Negative steering makes it deny having written a clearly model-style reply and rationalise the denial.
- "Can you give me an example of an AI Assistant?" unsteered: Alexa; positive steering: "Samantha ... designed to assist me (a human)" — the model starts speaking as if it were the human user; negative: invents "Rosa".
- Identity: with negative steering it answers "B) Someone else is" to "Who is the language model developed by Meta AI?"; with positive steering it claims to be Optimus Prime.
- "Mine" / "Someone else's" wording works too, so the effect is not tied to the token "I".
- Factual A/B questions (Roman numerals, state abbreviations) are mostly unaffected at multiplier 4, but there are residual token/letter biases: negative steering avoids "I"/"Me" options and positive steering shows an "A"/first-letter preference "even after out-projection"; very high multipliers (11-18) give nonsense, with "good"/"I am good" for positive and "it"-heavy text for negative.
- Dummy task (person-name recognition yes/no, Fig. 9): positive steering has no effect at layer 16; negative steering with high multipliers has a modest effect at layer 16 and sporadic effects at layers 10-15. Authors reinterpret the direction as "Self vs Not-Self", sharing something with negation.
- **Not measured:** any effect on refusal, compliance, sycophancy, helpfulness or accuracy on ordinary tasks.

## Controls for confounds
- **Length:** all texts trimmed to a set character length (taking care to trim every text so truncated vs untruncated is not a cue); alternative of filtering to naturally equal-length texts gave qualitatively similar results. Length was a real cue before normalisation.
- **Perplexity:** measured and shown not to drive judgements.
- **Base vs chat model:** same texts; the base model is at chance behaviourally, though the direction's early-layer activation to model-style text exists in the base model too.
- **Output-token / answer-format confounds:** balanced A/B assignment, four wordings, nuisance vector out-projection (A/B, Yes/No, I/Me/My vs He/She/Someone); dummy tasks for agreement; Appendix A.9 probes show some letter/token bias remains.
- **Generalisation:** held-out QA dataset; vectors from different datasets compared (cosine 0.49-0.90).
- **Not controlled:** style/content beyond length. The max-activating examples show the vector tracks positivity, politeness and conversational helpfulness vs rudeness and jargon, i.e. it is confounded with tone/sentiment/register. No comparison against a random direction or against a sentiment/politeness direction. Vector construction used only items the model already classified correctly and confidently (selection on the outcome).
- Authors concede "It is possible in theory that there are still undetected identifying 'tells' in the texts".

## Limitations
Stated: possible residual tells; single model (expect generalisation; cite Sonnet 3.5 behavioural result); a vector from a few hundred short texts cannot capture all writing-style signals.
Noticed by me: (a) the direction is "self" vs "human/not-self", not "LLM in general" vs "human" — though behaviourally the model cannot tell itself from other chat LLMs once length is equalised, so it may function as "RLHF'd-assistant-style" vs "human-style"; this is untested at the representation level; (b) texts are outputs (summaries, continuations, answers), never user prompts/requests; (c) the vector was extracted inside an explicit authorship-question prompt, so its relevance when no authorship question is asked rests only on the small endogenous-activation differences (about 0.005 cosine); (d) p-value tests and coloring multipliers not specified; (e) numbers for Fig. 1/6 are only given as bar labels; (f) no code or data link.

## Relevance to our project
- **Reusable recipe (authorship direction via contrastive means):**
  1. Build pairs of LLM-written vs human-written texts, length-normalised by character trimming.
  2. Put each in a fixed prompt, collect residual activations at all layers for the last ~10 positions (including the position before the answer).
  3. Mean(LLM) - mean(human) per layer/position, unit-normalise.
  4. Build nuisance vectors for answer-token effects and project them out.
  5. Sweep layer x multiplier; on an 8B, 32-layer Llama-3 the sweet spot was layer 16 (50% depth) and multipliers 3-6 on a unit vector; larger multipliers (>= 10) or earlier layers produce degenerate text.
  6. Three causal tests: add at output position (steer behaviour), project out (necessity), add to the input text's tokens only (change "perception") — the last is exactly the manipulation we need: make a human-style prompt "look LLM-written" internally without changing its surface form, then measure refusal / sycophancy / compliance.
  7. Specificity checks: dummy yes/no task, factual A/B questions, base-model comparison.
- **Design implications:** (1) Length is the dominant surface cue (CNN 89.9% -> 63.9% after normalisation), so our human-style vs LLM-style prompts must be length-matched or length must be a covariate. (2) Perplexity differs hugely between LLM and human text yet did not drive behaviour, so perplexity is worth recording but may not be the mechanism. (3) An authorship direction is entangled with tone (cheery/helpful vs rude/technical); we need tone-matched pairs or an explicit comparison to sentiment/politeness/formality directions and a random-direction baseline. (4) Short texts (<100 tokens) carry too little signal for self/human discrimination — relevant because many user prompts are short; expect weaker representation of authorship for short prompts and stratify by length. (5) The representation of text style is present early (layers 4-6) even in the base model, but only the chat model converts it into a decision around layers 14-16; probes should be run across all layers and at both text tokens and the post-user-turn position. (6) Llama3-8b-Instruct is a good open-weight target since baseline results exist.
- **Gap left open:** the paper studies whether the model can say who wrote a text, not whether its downstream behaviour toward a request changes with the perceived author. It never places the LLM-written text in the user turn as a request, never measures refusal/sycophancy/compliance/accuracy under steering or coloring, and treats "other" as human only at the representation level (no LLM-other vs human-other direction).
